## A. Executive Brief

The beauty service industry is undergoing a fundamental shift driven by Generation Z. For a high-school-aged entrepreneur like the founder of Nalani Luxe Hair, understanding how her peers discover, evaluate, and book hair services is not just useful — it is the entire product strategy. Gen Z clients do not browse; they choose from what algorithms show them, relying heavily on visual proof, authenticity, and frictionless mobile experiences. [1] [2] Furthermore, because the founder and many of her clients are minors, parent trust signals are equally important and must be built into the site's architecture from the start.

The research reveals five non-negotiable realities. First, **67% of Gen Z clients have walked away from a business entirely due to a bad booking system**, and over half say an active TikTok or Instagram presence is "very" or "extremely" important before they will book. [2] Second, **word of mouth still outperforms social media** as the top referral mechanism (25% vs. 20%), meaning the in-person experience and post-appointment follow-up are as important as the content strategy. [2] Third, **pricing transparency is a conversion multiplier** — clear pricing increases booking likelihood by two to three times compared to services with hidden or unclear costs. [1] Fourth, **mobile is not a consideration; it is the entire experience** — the booking flow, portfolio, and social proof must all be designed for a 375px screen first. [3] Fifth, **the founder's age is an asset, not a liability** — a high-school stylist serving her own generation creates an authenticity signal that no established salon can replicate, and the website should lean into this story rather than hide it.

### The Five Decisions to Make First

**Decision 1 — Mobile-First, Thumb-Friendly Architecture.** The website must be designed for mobile screens first, prioritizing fast load times (under three seconds), easy navigation within the "thumb zone," and a persistent "Book Now" call-to-action visible at all times. [3]

**Decision 2 — Transparent Pricing and Policies from Day One.** Clear service menus with upfront pricing, alongside straightforward cancellation and deposit policies, build immediate trust and reduce booking abandonment. A 50% deposit is the industry norm and should be collected at booking time. [4] [7]

**Decision 3 — Establish a Social-to-Site Growth Loop.** TikTok and Instagram should drive discovery and engagement; the website should close the booking. These two systems must reinforce each other with consistent branding, a direct link-in-bio, and content that creates urgency. [5]

**Decision 4 — Build Parent Trust Signals into the Site Architecture.** A dedicated "For Parents" section detailing the booking process, consent requirements, drop-off policies, and secure payment options is essential for the parent-assisted booking segment. [6]

**Decision 5 — Select a Booking Platform That Handles the Full Flow.** GlossGenius is the strongest recommendation for a solo independent stylist at this stage: it is beauty-industry-specific, mobile-first, supports intake forms with photo uploads, collects deposits automatically, and sends automated reminders — all with a predictable flat-rate subscription. [7]

## B. Audience Model

The following four profiles represent the primary user segments for Nalani Luxe Hair. Each profile is grounded in observed behavior, not assumption.

### Profile 1: The Independent Teen (Self-Booking)

This client is 14–18 years old, has her own phone, and makes her own beauty decisions. She discovered the stylist on TikTok or Instagram while searching for a specific style she saw on a creator. She has already decided she wants Bora Bora braids or a French curl bob before she ever visits the website — the website's job is to confirm she is in the right place and remove every obstacle between her and a completed booking. [8]

Her **motivations** are aesthetic and social: she wants to look good for school, an event, or simply for her social feed. She is budget-conscious — 72% of Gen Z cite cost as the most important purchase factor — but she will pay for quality if the visual proof is compelling. [4] Her **trust needs** are almost entirely visual: high-quality before-and-after photos, a consistent and active social presence, and pricing that is visible without requiring her to DM or call. Her **friction points** are speed and complexity — a slow-loading gallery, a multi-step form that requires account creation, or a booking flow that asks for too much information will cause her to abandon and look elsewhere. [2] Her **discovery path** is: TikTok search or Explore → Instagram profile → Link in bio → Booking page. She will rarely use Google as a first step.

### Profile 2: The Parent-Assisted Booker

This client is a parent or guardian, typically 35–50 years old, who is either booking on behalf of their teen or co-signing a booking their teen initiated. They may have found the stylist via Google, a local Facebook group, or because their teen showed them the Instagram profile. Their evaluation criteria are fundamentally different from the teen's: they are assessing **safety, professionalism, and reliability**, not aesthetic trend alignment.

Their **motivations** are practical: they need a reliable, safe, and professional environment for their child. They are managing logistics — transportation, payment, and scheduling around school and activities. Their **trust needs** include a professional-looking website, clear communication about what to expect, explicit policies regarding minors (drop-off rules, age requirements, consent), and a secure, recognizable payment gateway. Their **friction points** are ambiguity and informality — a website that looks like a social media page with no clear contact information, no policies, and no professional presentation will cause them to decline regardless of how much their teen wants to go. Their **discovery path** is often Google search or a direct referral from another parent.

### Profile 3: The Young Repeat Client

This client has already visited once and had a positive experience. She is now booking for maintenance (braid touch-ups, style refreshes) or for a specific upcoming event (homecoming, prom, graduation, birthday). She values the relationship she has built with the stylist and the consistency of the experience. Her **motivations** are loyalty and convenience — she wants to rebook quickly without re-entering all her information. Her **trust needs** are already met; she is primarily looking for availability. Her **friction points** are difficulty finding open slots, no automated rebooking prompt, and the absence of any loyalty recognition. Her **discovery path** is direct — she either bookmarks the site, uses a rebooking link from a previous confirmation text, or follows the stylist on social media and clicks the link when she is ready.

### Profile 4: The Event-Driven First-Timer

This client is booking specifically for a high-stakes event — prom, homecoming, graduation, a birthday party, or a vacation. She may have been referred by a friend who is already a client, or she may have found the stylist through a seasonal search or a trending post. She has a hard deadline and is more willing to pay a premium for a guaranteed slot. Her **motivations** are urgency and outcome — she needs her hair to look perfect on a specific date. Her **trust needs** are high: she needs to see examples of event-appropriate styles, understand the timeline for her appointment, and feel confident the stylist can deliver under pressure. Her **friction points** are availability uncertainty and unclear prep requirements. She needs to know immediately whether her desired date is available and what she needs to do to prepare.

## D. Website Requirements

The following requirements are organized by priority tier to guide the MVP build and subsequent iterations.

### Must Have for MVP

The website must open with a clear, mobile-optimized value proposition that communicates who the stylist is, what she specializes in, and who she serves — all visible without scrolling on a standard phone screen. A persistent "Book Now" button must be present at all times, either in the header or as a sticky bottom bar. [3]

The service menu must use current, recognizable style names (Knotless Box Braids, Goddess Braids, Fulani Braids, Boho Bobs, French Curl Braids) paired with base prices and estimated durations. Hidden pricing is one of the fastest ways to lose a Gen Z client. [1] [4] The portfolio must be a high-quality grid of real work — not stock photos — showing before-and-after results that match the styles listed in the service menu.

The booking integration must handle the full flow: service selection, date/time selection, intake form (including inspiration photo upload), deposit collection, and automated confirmation. The cancellation and no-show policy must be visible on the booking page and must require acknowledgment before payment is processed. [7]

### Strong Next-Step Enhancements

A dedicated "For Parents" section should be added as soon as the MVP is live. This section should address: what the booking process looks like, how payment works, what the drop-off and pick-up process is, and how to contact the stylist directly. This section is a direct conversion tool for the parent-assisted booking segment. [6]

An "About the Founder" section that leans into the founder's age and story is a significant trust builder. A high-school stylist serving her own generation is a compelling narrative that creates immediate relatability with teen clients and demonstrates credibility to parents. This section should include a professional photo, a brief personal statement, and a note about her training and experience.

Automated appointment reminders (24 hours before and 2 hours before) should be configured through the booking platform. These significantly reduce no-show rates and are expected by Gen Z clients who are accustomed to automated digital communication. [2]

### Future Ideas, Not Needed Now

A loyalty program (digital punch card or points system) is a strong long-term retention tool but adds complexity to the MVP. An e-commerce integration for selling recommended hair care products is a viable revenue stream but should wait until the booking business is stable. A waitlist feature for fully booked dates is valuable for high-demand periods like prom season and should be considered for Phase 2.

## E. Mobile Booking Flow Spec

The following spec defines the ideal step-by-step mobile booking flow. Each step is designed to minimize cognitive load, build trust, and reduce abandonment. This spec is intended to be directly translatable into Claude Code tasks.

**Step 1: Service Discovery and Selection**

The user's goal at this step is to identify and select the style they want. The screen should present a visual grid or list of service cards, each containing a high-quality image of the style, the service name (using current terminology), the starting price, and the estimated duration. The primary action is a large, easy-to-tap "Select" button. The trust signal at this step is the image itself — it must be a real photo of the stylist's work, not a stock image. The mobile UX note is to use cards large enough to be tapped without zooming, with clear visual hierarchy between the style name and the price. If a service is temporarily unavailable, it should be grayed out with a "Join Waitlist" option rather than removed entirely.

**Step 2: Date and Time Selection**

The user's goal is to find an available slot that fits their schedule. The screen should present a scrollable calendar view with available dates clearly marked and unavailable dates grayed out. Once a date is selected, available time slots should appear below. The primary action is tapping a specific time slot. The error state for a fully booked date should display a friendly message ("All slots for this date are taken — join the waitlist?") rather than a generic error. The trust signal is the real-time availability display, which communicates that the stylist's time is in demand. The mobile UX note is to ensure the calendar is large enough to tap individual dates without pinching, and that the time slots are displayed in a clear, scrollable list rather than a dropdown.

**Step 3: Client Details and Intake**

The user's goal is to provide the information needed to prepare for the appointment. The form should collect: name, phone number, email, a brief hair history (text field), hair length (dropdown), and an optional inspiration photo upload. The primary action is a "Continue to Payment" button. The trust signal is a brief note stating that information is kept private and used only to prepare for the appointment. The mobile UX note is critical: use appropriate keyboard types for each field (numeric for phone, email for email address), enable auto-fill, and keep the form to a maximum of six fields. The inspiration photo upload must pull from the phone's camera roll natively — do not require a separate upload step.

**Step 4: Deposit and Confirmation**

The user's goal is to secure the appointment by paying the deposit. The screen should display a clear summary: the selected service, date, time, total price, deposit amount (50% of total), and the balance due at the appointment. The cancellation policy must be displayed here with a required checkbox before the payment button becomes active. The primary action is "Confirm & Pay Deposit." The trust signal is the presence of recognized payment logos (Stripe, Square, Apple Pay, Google Pay). The mobile UX note is to support Apple Pay and Google Pay to eliminate the need to manually enter card details, which is the single biggest friction point in mobile checkout for Gen Z. [2]

**Step 5: Post-Booking Confirmation**

Immediately after a successful deposit, the user should see a confirmation screen with: the appointment details, a calendar add button, and a note about what to expect next (e.g., "You'll receive a reminder 24 hours before your appointment"). An automated SMS should be sent within two minutes containing the appointment summary and prep instructions. This step is not optional — it is the first impression of the stylist's professionalism and the primary driver of appointment show rates.

## F. Social-to-Site Growth Loop

### The Core Loop

The growth system for Nalani Luxe Hair operates as a closed loop where each stage feeds the next. The loop begins with content creation and ends with a client who has been converted into a brand ambassador.

**Attract → Direct → Convert → Delight → Retain → Amplify → Attract**

Content posted on TikTok and Instagram (Attract) generates views from the target audience. Clear CTAs in captions and video text direct viewers to the link in bio (Direct). The link leads to a fast, mobile-optimized booking page with transparent pricing (Convert). An excellent in-person service experience creates a delighted client (Delight). Automated follow-up texts prompt a Google review and offer a rebooking link (Retain). The client posts her new hair on social media, tagging the stylist (Amplify), which generates new discovery for the next potential client (Attract again). [5]

The critical insight here is that the website is not the beginning of the funnel — it is the middle. Social media handles discovery; the website handles conversion and trust. Neither can do the other's job.

### Platform Strategy

**TikTok** is the primary discovery engine for Gen Z clients. It functions as a search engine for this generation — users actively search "knotless braids near me" or "boho bob tutorial" and discover stylists through algorithmic recommendations. TikTok content should prioritize transformation videos (before-and-after), process videos showing the braiding technique, and personality-driven content that builds connection. The algorithm rewards early engagement, so posting during peak hours and responding to comments quickly is essential. [5]

**Instagram** functions as the portfolio and credibility layer. After discovering a stylist on TikTok, Gen Z clients almost universally check the Instagram profile before booking. The grid should be curated to show a range of styles, and the bio should contain a direct booking link (not a Linktree with multiple options — one clear link). Stories and Reels serve a secondary discovery function and should mirror the TikTok content strategy. [2]

**Google Business Profile** is the parent-facing discovery channel. Parents searching for a hair stylist for their teen will use Google, not TikTok. A complete and regularly updated Google Business Profile with photos, hours, and a booking link is essential for this segment.

### 30-Day Content and Testing Framework

The first 30 days should be used to establish content patterns and identify what resonates with the specific audience before investing in a high-volume posting schedule.

**Week 1 — Authority and Education.** Post two to three pieces of educational content: a tutorial on how to maintain braids at home, an explanation of the difference between knotless and traditional box braids, or a "what to expect at your first appointment" video. This content positions the stylist as knowledgeable and builds trust before the audience has seen her work.

**Week 2 — Transformations.** Post two to three high-quality before-and-after Reels or TikToks featuring trending styles (Bora Bora braids, French curl bob, Boho Bob). These are the highest-converting content type for hair stylists and should be the core of the content strategy. [8] Each video should include a clear CTA: "Link in bio to book."

**Week 3 — Behind the Scenes.** Post two to three pieces of process content: time-lapse of a braid installation, a "day in the life" clip, or a candid moment from an appointment. This content builds the personal connection that Gen Z values and differentiates the stylist from larger, more corporate salons.

**Week 4 — Social Proof and Seasonal Urgency.** Share one to two client testimonials (with permission) and announce limited availability for an upcoming seasonal event (homecoming, back-to-school, etc.). Scarcity messaging ("Only 3 spots left for September") is highly effective for driving immediate bookings. [9]

### Content That Lives on Social vs. the Website

Content that belongs on social media includes: raw transformation videos, process clips, trending audio content, polls and questions, Stories updates, and personality-driven content. Content that belongs on the website includes: the full portfolio with high-resolution photos, detailed service descriptions and pricing, policies, the booking flow, the founder story, and parent-facing information. The website is the authoritative source of record; social media is the discovery and engagement layer.

### Seasonal Booking Calendar

Understanding the seasonal demand calendar allows for proactive content planning and strategic availability management. The following periods represent the highest-demand windows for a teen-focused stylist.

**Back-to-School (Late July – Early September):** The first major booking surge of the year. Clients want fresh protective styles that will last through the first weeks of school. Content should begin three to four weeks before school starts. This is also a strong period for parent-assisted bookings.

**Homecoming (September – October):** A high-stakes event for high school clients. Demand for updo-adjacent styles, Goddess braids, and polished looks spikes sharply. Limited availability messaging is highly effective during this window.

**Holiday Season (November – December):** Family gatherings, holiday parties, and New Year's events drive demand for polished, event-ready styles. This is also a strong period for gift card sales if that feature is implemented.

**Prom and Graduation (April – June):** The highest-stakes booking period of the year for a high-school-focused stylist. Clients book weeks in advance, and the stylist's own social circle becomes a direct referral network. Waitlist management is essential during this period.

**Summer (June – August):** Strong demand for vacation-ready protective styles (Bora Bora braids, Boho Bobs) that can last four to eight weeks. This is the best period for building the portfolio and social media presence.

### Visual Language and Design Cues

The visual identity of the website and social channels should feel current without becoming disposable. The following design principles are grounded in what resonates with Gen Z in 2025–2026. [3] [10]

Clean, modern typography with personality — not corporate sans-serifs, but not overly decorative either. A color palette that is warm, confident, and feminine without being pink-and-gold cliché. Dark mode support is a strong signal of design sophistication. Micro-interactions (subtle animations on hover or tap) add delight without slowing the experience. Vertical video is the default format for all media content. Auto-playing video (muted) on the homepage hero section is highly effective for hair stylists because it shows the work in motion.

### Trends to Avoid

Several patterns are oversaturated, difficult to maintain, or likely to age badly. Generic "luxury" branding (excessive gold, marble textures, script fonts) has become a cliché in the independent stylist space and reads as inauthentic to Gen Z. Overly filtered or heavily edited portfolio photos undermine trust — this generation prefers real, unfiltered results. Complicated multi-step booking flows with mandatory account creation are a direct conversion killer. [2] Posting only finished styles without process content misses the engagement opportunity that process videos provide.

## H. Competitive Teardown

The following analysis examines patterns across young independent stylists, Gen Z beauty creators, high-performing braider websites, and mobile-first booking experiences. The goal is to extract reusable patterns, not to catalog specific competitors.

### Pattern 1: The "Link in Bio" Conversion Funnel

**What works:** The most effective independent stylists use a single, direct link in their social bio that leads to a mobile-optimized booking page — not a Linktree, not a homepage, not an Instagram grid. The link goes directly to the service selection step of the booking flow.

**What does not work:** Multiple links in bio (Linktree with five options) create decision paralysis. A link to a generic homepage that requires three more taps to reach the booking flow loses clients at each step.

**What is transferable:** Design the link-in-bio destination as a standalone mobile landing page that shows the top three services, a brief value proposition, and a single "Book Now" button. This page should load in under two seconds.

**Risk:** If the booking platform is slow or requires account creation, the conversion benefit of the direct link is lost.

### Pattern 2: Visual Service Menus with Real Work

**What works:** High-performing braider websites pair each service name with a real photo of the stylist's work, a starting price, and an estimated duration. This eliminates the most common source of client hesitation: uncertainty about whether the stylist can actually execute the desired style.

**What does not work:** Generic stock photos, low-resolution images, or photos that do not match the style being described. This is immediately detected by Gen Z clients and destroys trust.

**What is transferable:** Build the service menu as a visual card grid, not a text list. Each card should contain: a real photo, the style name, the starting price, and the estimated duration. The "Select" button should be the only CTA on the card.

**Risk:** If the portfolio is thin (early in the business), use a small number of high-quality photos rather than a large number of mediocre ones.

### Pattern 3: The Founder Story as a Trust Signal

**What works:** Independent stylists who share their personal story — especially when that story is distinctive (young, self-taught, serving their own community) — build a level of connection that no established salon can replicate. Gen Z values authenticity above almost all other brand signals. [10]

**What does not work:** A generic "About Me" section that reads like a corporate bio. Overly formal language, stock headshots, and generic statements about "passion for hair" are immediately recognized as inauthentic.

**What is transferable:** Write the founder story in the first person, in the founder's actual voice. Include a real photo (not a professional headshot — a natural, candid photo works better for this audience). Mention the founder's age and the fact that she is serving her own generation. This is a competitive advantage, not a liability.

**Risk:** The story must be genuine. If it reads as performed or curated, it will backfire with a Gen Z audience.

### Pattern 4: Transparent Policies as a Conversion Tool

**What works:** Stylists who display their cancellation, deposit, and no-show policies clearly — and who frame them as mutual respect rather than punitive measures — experience fewer disputes and higher booking completion rates. Gen Z clients appreciate knowing the rules upfront. [7]

**What does not work:** Hiding policies in a footer link, using aggressive or threatening language, or presenting policies as a long legal document.

**What is transferable:** Display the key policy points (deposit amount, cancellation window, no-show fee) on the booking page itself, not just in a separate policies section. Require a checkbox acknowledgment before the payment button activates. Frame the policy as protecting both parties: "Your deposit secures your slot and protects my time. Cancellations with 48+ hours notice receive a full refund."

**Risk:** An overly strict policy can deter first-time bookings. Consider a slightly more flexible policy for first-time clients and a stricter one for repeat no-shows.

### Pattern 5: Seasonal Urgency Messaging

**What works:** Stylists who announce limited availability for high-demand periods (prom, homecoming, graduation) well in advance — and who use specific scarcity messaging ("Only 4 slots remaining for May") — drive significantly higher advance bookings. [9]

**What does not work:** Generic "book now" messaging without context. Claiming false scarcity is immediately detected and destroys trust.

**What is transferable:** Build a seasonal content calendar that begins promoting high-demand periods four to six weeks in advance. Use the booking platform's waitlist feature to capture demand that exceeds capacity. Post the remaining slot count on social media as availability decreases.

**Risk:** If the stylist is not actually fully booked, claiming scarcity will undermine credibility when clients see open slots.

## I. Implications for Claude Code

This section translates the research findings into specific, actionable directives for the build. Each item is specific enough to convert into a GitHub issue, a component, or an acceptance criterion.

### Recommended Information Architecture

The site should have five primary pages, each with a clear, single purpose.

**Home** serves as the first impression and conversion entry point. It should contain: a hero section with a value proposition headline, a high-quality image or auto-playing muted video, and a sticky "Book Now" CTA; a "Featured Services" section showing the top three to four trending styles with visual cards; a brief "About the Founder" teaser with a link to the full story; and a social proof section with three to five recent testimonials.

**Services & Pricing** is the decision-support page. It should contain a visual grid of all services, organized by category (Braids, Twists, Maintenance, Event Styles), each with a photo, name, starting price, duration, and "Select" button that initiates the booking flow.

**Portfolio** is the trust-building page. It should be an Instagram-style grid of high-quality before-and-after photos, filterable by style category. Each photo should be full-resolution and show the stylist's real work.

**Policies & FAQ** is the objection-handling page. It should contain: a clear cancellation and deposit policy (with friendly, non-aggressive language), a no-show policy, prep instructions per service category, and a dedicated "For Parents" section.

**About** is the connection-building page. It should contain the founder's full story, a real photo, a brief note about her training and experience, and a link to her social profiles.

### Component Architecture

The following components should be built as reusable, independently testable units.

| Component | Props | Notes |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| `ServiceCard` | `image`, `name`, `startingPrice`, `duration`, `onSelect` | Used on Services page and Home featured section. |
| `BookingFlow` | `selectedService`, `onComplete` | Multi-step wizard: Service → Date/Time → Intake → Payment. |
| `PortfolioGrid` | `photos[]`, `filterCategories[]` | Lazy-loaded, filterable image grid. |
| `PolicyAccordion` | `sections[]` | Collapsible policy sections. Keep each section concise. |
| `TestimonialSlider` | `testimonials[]` | Auto-rotating, touch-swipeable on mobile. |
| `StickyBookingCTA` | `href`, `label` | Fixed bottom bar on mobile; disappears on desktop. |
| `WaitlistForm` | `serviceId`, `date` | Triggered when a date is fully booked. |

### Data Model Considerations

The following data structures should be defined early to ensure the booking flow and portfolio are built on a consistent foundation.

A **Service** object should contain: `id`, `name`, `category`, `description`, `basePrice`, `duration` (in minutes), `imageUrl`, `isAvailable` (Boolean), and `addOns[]` (array of optional add-ons with name and price).

An **Appointment** object should contain: `id`, `clientId`, `serviceId`, `date`, `time`, `status` (enum: `pending`, `confirmed`, `completed`, `cancelled`, `no_show`), `depositPaid` (Boolean), `depositAmount`, `totalAmount`, `intakeData` (object containing hair history, inspiration photo URL, and notes), and `remindersSent[]`.

A **Client** object should contain: `id`, `name`, `phone`, `email`, `hairHistory`, `bookingHistory[]`, and `parentContact` (optional, for minor clients).

### Booking-State Requirements

The booking flow must handle the following states gracefully. Real-time availability must be checked before confirming a time slot — two users should not be able to book the same slot simultaneously. A time slot should be temporarily locked (for 10 minutes) while a user completes the intake form and payment, and released if the booking is not completed. A successful payment state should trigger: an appointment confirmation record, an automated SMS to the client, and a calendar event creation option. A failed payment state should display a clear, non-alarming error message and allow the user to retry without losing their selected service and time slot.

### Mobile Interaction Notes

All tap targets must be at least 44×44 pixels, per Apple's Human Interface Guidelines. The booking flow should use a bottom-sheet modal pattern on mobile rather than a full-page navigation, to maintain context. Forms must use appropriate keyboard types: `type="tel"` for phone numbers, `type="email"` for email addresses, `inputmode="numeric"` for numeric fields. The inspiration photo upload must use the native file picker with `accept="image/*"` and `capture="environment"` to allow direct camera access. The calendar date picker should be a custom component, not the native browser date input, which renders inconsistently across mobile browsers.

### Experiments Worth Running After Launch

**Experiment 1 — CTA Copy Test.** Test "Book Now" vs. "Secure Your Spot" vs. "Check Availability" as the primary CTA label. Hypothesis: "Check Availability" reduces perceived commitment and increases click-through rate from hesitant first-time visitors.

**Experiment 2 — Intake Form Timing.** Test asking for the inspiration photo during the booking flow vs. in a post-booking follow-up email. Hypothesis: Moving the photo upload to a follow-up email reduces form abandonment while still capturing the information before the appointment.

**Experiment 3 — Pricing Display Format.** Test showing exact prices (e.g., "$120") vs. "Starting at" prices (e.g., "Starting at $80") for complex styles. Hypothesis: "Starting at" pricing increases click-through to the service detail page but may increase abandonment at checkout if the final price is significantly higher.

**Experiment 4 — Social Proof Placement.** Test placing testimonials on the booking page itself (near the payment step) vs. only on the home page. Hypothesis: Testimonials at the payment step reduce abandonment by addressing last-minute hesitation.

**Experiment 5 — Parent FAQ Visibility.** Test a dedicated "For Parents" link in the main navigation vs. a section embedded on the FAQ page. Hypothesis: A dedicated nav link increases engagement from parent visitors and reduces inbound inquiries about policies.
